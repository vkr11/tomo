# Story: Ask for Help Sooner (38-Person Team)

## 10-second hook (BLUF)
I tried to carry a 38-person cross-functional team through a critical graph initiative with two open PM roles I hadn't filled — quality slipped, and my manager told me I should have asked for help sooner. I owned it, restructured how the team operated, hired the two PMs, and we delivered the Real World Friend Graph at >1% MAU impact for Facebook and Instagram.

## STAR+ (default delivery — S 10% · T 10% · A 60% · R 20%)

**Situation** — The Real World Friend Graph sat underneath some of Meta's most important growth and integrity experiences, powering connection and relevance across FB, IG, and Messenger. I was leading a 38-person cross-functional team, and I was short two PMs I was supposed to have hired.

**Task** — I owned delivery of the graph initiative *and* the staffing gap. My instinct was to absorb the missing PM scope myself rather than slow the roadmap.

**Action** — That instinct was the mistake, and the honest version of this story starts there. By trying to personally cover two roles across a 38-person team, I spread myself thin — I dropped balls and shipped some work at lower quality than the bar. What turned it around:
- When my manager named it directly — "you should have asked for help earlier" — I took the feedback at face value instead of defending it.
- I stopped trying to do everything and instead **defined a prioritized cut of the work**, then got fast alignment with the team and leadership on what we would and wouldn't do given the gap.
- I built the operating scaffolding the team had been missing — clear agendas, an operating cadence, decision-making structure — so the team scaled on process rather than on my personal bandwidth.
- I closed the actual gap: I defined the two PM profiles I needed, hired them, and onboarded them into the structure I'd built.

**Result** — The team stabilized and we delivered the Real World Friend Graph at **>1% MAU impact for Facebook and Instagram**, powering roughly **5% of all Messenger conversations**. The durable win wasn't the metric — it was that I stopped being the single point of failure for a 38-person org.
